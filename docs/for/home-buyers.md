# BnBDetector for Home Buyers

Buying is harder to undo than a lease. The BnBIndex will not tell you what the flat is worth. It will tell you whether active Airbnb listings already cluster around the address, on a 0–100 scale, before you write an offer or waive a condition.

Buyers use that in two ways. Some want a residential building and need to see guest turnover before they own the problem. Some are comfortable with a tourist building and still need the facts, because resale to the next residential buyer gets harder when the block feels like lodging. BnBDetector is the activity check. An appraiser and a host-revenue model answer other questions.

## What to run, and when

Run the building address as soon as it is a real candidate, not after the offer is emotional. Run it again before you are bound, if time has passed. Max and Ultra include one free reCheck per address each month. A score from the first weekend of a search is not automatically the score at closing.

Read both numbers:

- The nearby Airbnb count, about an 80-meter radius in the current product.
- The BnBIndex band: 0–30 residential signal, 31–58 mixed, 59–100 heavy.

A handful of listings in a large city building can be ordinary (Residential Core). A score in the Airbnb Dominated band means the immediate area is thick with listings. That is a lifestyle fact and a resale fact. It is not, by itself, a valuation.

## Documents the score does not replace

- The condo or HOA rules, especially minimum lease terms and short-stay bans. Many associations restrict stays. Whether they enforce the rule is a separate question, and a high BnBIndex is a reason to ask it.
- Local licensing, night caps, and taxes. The report does not look up permits. Start with the city’s own pages. The site’s regulation guides are background: https://www.bnbdetector.com/en/guides/regulations
- The inspection, the title, and the price. None of those live in the BnBIndex.
- A revenue estimate, if the purchase only works as a short-term rental. That is an AirDNA-style question, and it assumes hosting is allowed. See [BnBDetector vs AirDNA](../vs/airdna.md).

If the rules ban short stays and the score is high, you are looking at a gap between the paper and the listings. Ask the board or the manager what they do about it. Bring the date, the score, and the count. Do not describe the report as a city enforcement record.

## How a buyer should read awkward cases

**Low score, tourist city.** Possible, and useful. Famous neighborhoods are not uniform. An 18 on the street you would actually own is more informative than the city’s reputation.

**High score, beautiful flat.** The flat can still be beautiful. Budget for guest traffic, or keep shopping. The score is telling you the surroundings, not insulting the renovation.

**Low score, and you hoped to host.** A quiet result means few Airbnb listings were detected nearby. It says nothing about whether you are allowed to list, or what you would earn.

**Score from a nearby landmark.** Check the building address, not the neighborhood name. Eighty meters is the building and its immediate neighbors.

## A buying sequence

1. When a listing becomes a finalist, run the address on BnBDetector.
2. If it is 59 or above, decide explicitly whether you want to own in that kind of area.
3. Read the building rules the same day.
4. Visit the common areas, not only the unit. Go at an hour when guests move if you can.
5. ReCheck before you remove contingencies or send the final funds.
6. Write down the address, the date, the score, and the count. Quote the report narrowly.

Prices and plan size are on https://www.bnbdetector.com/en/pricing. One purchase decision is often a Starter pack of 10 reports. A search across many buildings fits Max.

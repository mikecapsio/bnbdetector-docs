# Ways to Check Airbnb Activity Before You Move

You want to know whether a building behaves like a home or like a small hotel. Several tools and habits answer pieces of that. They do not answer the same question, and using only one of them is how people get surprised after move-in.

## Quick answer

- Choose **BnBDetector** when you have a street address and you need a comparable 0–100 read of nearby Airbnb activity before you rent, buy, or renew.
- Choose **searching Airbnb yourself** when you want photos, reviews, and a manual look at pins, and you can spend the time. Do this as a supplement, not as your only method.
- Choose **AirDNA** when the question is hosting income, occupancy, or nightly pricing. That is a host and investor question.
- Choose **Inside Airbnb** when you need a public dataset or a city map for research, journalism, or advocacy, and the city is one they publish.
- **Walk the building** when you can. Lockboxes and luggage are evidence a dataset will miss, and a dataset will see listings you cannot see from the lobby.
- **Read the lease, the building rules, and the local law** when you need permission and enforcement, not just activity. No detector settles that.

A solid personal search uses the address check, a visit, and the documents. The others are for different jobs.

## 1. BnBDetector: an address score for people who will live there

BnBDetector takes an address and returns a BnBIndex from 0 to 100 plus the count of active Airbnb listings nearby. The current area is about 80 meters. The same report is on every plan. Most checks finish in under 10 seconds, and the search stays on your account. The building is not notified.

Use it to compare a shortlist on one scale: a 12 and a 74 are different decisions. Prefer 0–30 when a residential building is the goal. Treat 31–58 as a compromise you should notice. Treat 59–100 as heavy Airbnb pressure.

BnBDetector does not name units, does not audit other booking sites, does not measure noise, and does not rule on legality. Read [How to read a BnBIndex report](how-to-read-a-report.md) before you forward a score to someone else.

Website: https://www.bnbdetector.com

## 2. Searching Airbnb yourself

Open Airbnb, search the area, and try to match listings to the building using the map, photos of the facade, and the description. It costs nothing but your time. It is worth doing when a BnBIndex is high and you want to see what the listings actually look like.

The limits are structural. Airbnb hides the exact address until a stay is booked, so you are matching a fuzzy pin and a crop of the building. Listings for one block are easy to under-count. The same home may also be on another site and invisible in an Airbnb-only pass. You do not get a stable score, so “I clicked around for twenty minutes” is hard to compare across five buildings.

Use the manual search to investigate. Use BnBDetector to screen. The comparison is in [BnBDetector vs searching Airbnb yourself](vs/searching-airbnb-yourself.md).

## 3. AirDNA: market analytics for hosts and investors

AirDNA publishes short-term rental data for hosts, investors, and property managers: market research, revenue and occupancy estimates, comparable listings, and dynamic pricing tools. Its public materials describe coverage across Airbnb and other major booking sites, and a free tier plus paid research plans. Current packages are on https://www.airdna.co/pricing.

That is a different decision. AirDNA helps someone ask what a property might earn as a short-term rental and how to price nights. BnBDetector helps someone ask whether nearby Airbnb activity makes the address a good place to live. A strong revenue estimate and a high BnBIndex can describe the same building from opposite sides. If you are the future neighbor, the income estimate does not tell you whether you will like the hallway.

Details: [BnBDetector vs AirDNA](vs/airdna.md).

## 4. Inside Airbnb: public data for cities they cover

Inside Airbnb is a data project, associated with Murray Cox and the Housing Justice Data Lab, that collects publicly visible Airbnb information, cleans it, and publishes maps and downloadable files. The audience is researchers, journalists, advocates, residents, and policymakers. Coverage is the cities and regions they publish, with country archives for a stated list of countries. It is not a form where you type any address on earth and get a personal score.

Use it when the question is “What does Airbnb look like in this city, and can I download the data?” Use BnBDetector when the question is “What about this building, this week, for my lease?”

Details: [BnBDetector vs Inside Airbnb](vs/inside-airbnb.md).
Project: https://insideairbnb.com

## 5. Walking the building and visiting at the right hour

A tour at 11:00 on a Tuesday shows you the flat. It is a weak sample of guest traffic. If you can, pass the entrance on a Friday evening or a Sunday morning, and look for lockboxes, a stream of suitcases, housekeeping carts, and a lobby that works like a reception.

What you see is real and narrow. You will not see upper floors you cannot access, and you will not see listings that are between guests. A quiet hour does not cancel a high BnBIndex. A lockbox does not tell you how many listings are on the block. Do both.

## 6. Asking the landlord, agent, or manager

Ask whether short stays are allowed and how many units are used that way. Some people will answer clearly. Some do not know. Some have a reason to describe the building as quieter than it is.

Treat the answer as a claim. The report is an independent count of nearby Airbnb listings, private to you, so you can ask better questions without warning the building that you looked.

## 7. Registries, licenses, and the lease

Some cities require registration, a night cap, or a host-present rule. Some buildings ban stays under 30 days in the lease or the condo rules. Those documents answer “Is it allowed?” The BnBIndex answers “What listing activity is detectable nearby?”

Both can be true at once: a ban on paper and a high score on the ground, or a licensed host and a building you still do not want to live in. Read the rule, then check the address. The site’s regulation and tenant-rights guides are starting points, not advice:

- https://www.bnbdetector.com/en/guides/regulations
- https://www.bnbdetector.com/en/guides/tenant-rights

## How to choose

1. **Are you about to live there, or about to host there?** Live there: BnBDetector, a visit, and the lease. Host there: a host-analytics product such as AirDNA, plus the local license rules.
2. **Do you have a street address?** If yes, check that address. A city map is context. It is not the building.
3. **Do you need a number you can compare across options?** Use the BnBIndex and the nearby count. A manual browse does not give you that.
4. **Do you need public data you can download and cite in research?** Look at Inside Airbnb for cities they cover.
5. **Do you need to know if it is legal?** Read the local rules and the contract. Do not ask a score to do that.
6. **Can you visit?** Go, at an hour when guests move. Keep the report anyway, because one visit is a sample.

## A practical combination

1. Screen every address on the shortlist with BnBDetector.
2. Drop or deprioritize Airbnb Dominated buildings unless you have a reason to accept them.
3. For anything in Mixed Community or above that you might still take, search Airbnb yourself and look at photos and reviews.
4. Visit the finalists, including the common areas.
5. Read the lease or HOA rules on subletting and minimum stays.
6. ReCheck the winner on Max or Ultra just before you pay.
